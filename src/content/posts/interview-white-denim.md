A Texan band in Berlin. This could be the basis for a B-movie, but last week, this is exactly what went down, when Austin-based [White Denim](http://whitedenimmusic.com/) headlined Berlin’s [Magnet Club](http://www.magnet-club.de/) on tour in support of its new album(s).

Since its formation half a decade ago, White Denim has been operating at maximum capacity, releasing an album (or two) a year. The band’s most recent full-length, “D,” was released earlier this year.

But although it was the fourth studio full-length White Denim made, “D” was actually the fifth album the band has released.

Taking a break from the label game, the band – which had just changed from a three-piece to a four-piece with the addition of guitarist Austin Jenkins – spent last summer recording “Last Day of Summer” at drummer Josh Block’s home.

“\[The albums are\] actually really similar, except for the production,” bassist Steve Terebecki said from his position on a sofa in the club, prior to the evening's show. “It was sort of the same time period and the same method. But just completely different styles.”

This is because “Last Day of Summer” wasn’t recorded at a professional studio. Instead, the album was self-produced and self-released, and offered for free download on the White Denim website.

All the members contribute to writing their parts of a song, and with rare exception, singer and guitarist James Petralli pens all the words. Still, the other members don’t feel any disconnect with what he writes.

“I really enjoy his lyrics, I think they’re really really cool,” Jenkins said, looking up from restringing his guitar. “He has a lot of different styles that he can write in that I think is really exciting. At least to me, it’s like, he can tell stories, sort of kind of be, for lack of a better term, like poetic…I’m always really interested in the stuff because I think it kind of changes the meaning of the music.”

Terebecki agreed, noting that while the personal nature of some lyrics might not always resonate with the other members, the other three still hold Petralli’s writing in high regard.

“Even the really personal songs \[are\] good enough where even if it might be sort of corny, it means something to \[Petralli\] and you can’t really take that away from him,” he said.

As to whether or not the content of the songs can emotionally effect the other members, both Jenkins and Terebecki said it depends on a variety of factors, but typically, that they respond to different things.

“We have songs that have just lots of parts, that are fast,” Terebecki explained. “And I think \[on\] those songs it’s easier to forget what \[Petralli is\] saying, cause you’re really just focusing on shredding on your instrument.”

Jenkins agreed, but added that at different times, songs mean different things to him, and more often than not, they are influenced by the way in which Petralli performs them.

“Sometimes…I can hear it in a totally different way, or the meaning is kind of changed, you know what I mean? And then when you go to play it on stage again, like, it feels…almost like a new song,” Jenkins said. “\[And\] I hear tone, or how he’s delivering it, as opposed to like the exactness of the speech, you know? So that kind of informs you too. Like if he goes lighter on a verse or something like that, you have the tendency to kind of like \[play lighter\]. It’s a lighter moment.”

It's that attention to detail and to one another that contributes to White Denim's success – while playing the music live demands a lot from each member, they all realize the important of remaining in tune with one another, both on stage and off.

This synchronicity is also apparent in the interpersonal dynamic between the four, who admit they are more likely to argue about whether 311 or Sublime is better than to bicker with one another over matters directly concerning the band.

“When we first started we were literally just doing it for fun…everything was centered around having fun and drinking,” Terebecki said. “And now it’s a full-blown job where we have to be really responsible and like think really…we have to be thoughtful about every decision we make in the band.”

Part of that decision-making process also entails who the band wants to work with on a business level.

“\[We are interested in\] people who have a forward-thinking idea but are still grounded in what’s going on,” Jenkins said, explaining how sharing a similar vision is of the utmost importance, when it comes to the business side of things.

But business matters aside, what it eventually comes down to is how much fun the four have touring and playing the music.

“If I had to pick a favourite \[aspect\] I’d have to say traveling and playing would have to take it over being a studio,” Terebecki said. “All of us have been at the end of a rope…but that’s why we stay together, just because we enjoy playing music so much."

Jenkins agreed, but specified that he can’t fully recognize the value of the traveling without experiencing the other facets of being in a band as well.

“You don’t appreciate the traveling as much until you’ve been, kind of like, locked up working on something for a long time,” he explained. “So I think there’s a balance. But I really enjoy getting out and playing for the people and seeing different places and, like, being surrounded by other musicians.”
